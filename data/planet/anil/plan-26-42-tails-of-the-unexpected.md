<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#chasing-your-own-list-tail" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Chasing your own (list) tail</h3>
<p>As happens every year, there's a new and interesting question raised during
lectures that I don't anticipate. This year, it's how the OCaml head
and tail of a list work.</p>
<p>In lecture 3, I explained how lists are implemented as a singly-linked data
structure, starting from a head value and ending in an empty list sentinel.
The type of <code>val hd : 'a list -&gt; 'a</code> makes it obvious that the function needs
to raise an exception if the input list is empty, since a value of <code>'a</code> cannot
otherwise be constructed.  However, the question was why the tail function <code>val tl : 'a list -&gt; 'a list</code> can't just return an empty list instead of raising an
exception?</p>
<p>I had to look this one up, and couldn't find any language for which <code>tail([])=[]</code>, although GHC does have versions which don't raise. Talking to <a href="https://simon.peytonjones.org/">Simon Peyton Jones</a>, he reckons that we shouldn't have a <code>List.tl</code> function in the first place, and should always prefer pattern matching to deconstruct a list. There were <a href="https://x.com/avsm/status/2112124094636605574">lots</a> of other opinions online too.</p>

<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#ai-governance-with-csap" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>AI governance with CSaP</h3>
<p><img src="https://anil.recoil.org/images/w42-1.webp" alt="%rc" title="Me with Keita Azuma from the Japanese government">
The <a href="https://www.csap.cam.ac.uk">Centre for Science and Policy</a> here organises '<a href="https://www.csap.cam.ac.uk/policy-fellowships/policy-fellows/">policy fellow</a>' meetings for academics to interact directly with civil servants from around the world. I had the pleasure of meeting with <a href="https://unu.edu/cris/about/expert/keita-azuma">Keita Azuma</a> from Japan’s Cabinet Secretariat this week.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Keita Azuma is Chief Policy Officer for Emerging Technologies and Global Affairs in the Office of the Cabinet Secretary for Crisis Management, Government of Japan. He leads Japan's strategic engagement with international AI governance, coordinating closely with the United Nations and the OECD, including the UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies, UNODA, UNDP and UNU, and served as lead organiser of side events at the STI Forum.</p>
<p>[...] Within Japan's crisis management architecture, he has played a central role in integrating emerging technologies — including satellite imagery, AI analytics and drone-based reconnaissance — into the government's response to large-scale disasters, accidents and security crises, and has worked on countering mis- and disinformation during such events.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Keita and I had a really wide-ranging discussion ranging from the rollout of LLM technologies for policymaking (as we are working on in the <a href="https://evidencetap.org">Evidence TAP</a>) through to how forecasting models like the latest advances in <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/projects/tessera">Tessera</a> might apply to climate adaptation strategies in Japan.  I also learnt that Japan is hoping to host the <a href="https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/politics/politics-government/20251219-299398/">2028 AI summit</a> after next year's in Switzerland. After my <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/india-ai-summit">whirlwind Indian AI summit experience</a> earlier this year, I definitely want to attend that one!</p>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#the-conservation-research-institute-management-team" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>The Conservation Research Institute management team</h3>
<p>Cambridge is full of acronyms, and none more so than the <a href="https://www.cambridgeconservation.org">Cambridge Conservation Initiative</a> (CCI), home of <a href="https://www.unep-wcmc.org">UNEP-WCMC</a>, <a href="https://iucn.org">IUCN</a>, <a href="https://www.rspb.org.uk">RSPB</a> and of course the <a href="https://www.conservation.cam.ac.uk">CRI</a>. The CRI is the codename for the University members of the CCI, and we have regular meetings about how best to organise the interdisciplinary research. My attendance has been sporadic in the last year as I was on sabbatical (except for the fantastic <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/2026w19">CRI offsite in Norwich</a>) but I very much enjoyed seeing everyone again!</p>

<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#mcsamosas-at-pembroke" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>McSamosas at Pembroke</h3>
<p>Last but not least, this term as part of the <a href="https://evidencetap.org">Evidence TAP</a> project kicking off at Pembroke, I've been hosting 'samosa socials'. We're working across so many departments that many of our collaborators never really get to see each other in person. One of the great things about the College system is that we can quite easily find a room and invite 20-30 people without issue.</p>
<p>Our wonderful catering team led by Nina Rhodes has been supplying a steady stream of samosas, pakoras and chai, and we've made this a regular event in Michaelmas every Monday at 4pm. I expected numbers to dip after the first one, but we ended up with over 40 (!) people from 8 or more departments showing up. Discussions ranged from the AI safety announcement I mention above, to how we might locally help with the <a href="https://eleanortoyescott.co.uk/blog/posts/river-rights.html">Cam rights of the river</a>, to how a policymaking platform with LLMs could be crafted to fit how politicians work (messaging!), and how <a href="https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/carl/climate/">global cooperative policymaking</a> might be implemented. I am delighted to see many of the Pembroke undergrads attending as well, so I'm hopeful we can get some <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/cambridge-green-blue">Cambridge Green Blue</a> activities going next term if the momentum continues to build!</p>
