<p>The following aims to be a record (and advertisement!) of my recent work on observability in the Dune build&nbsp;system.</p><p>A bit about me: <a href="https://nat.karmios.com/">I’m Nat</a>, and I’m usually a <a href="https://vtss.doc.ic.ac.uk/">formal methods PhD student at Imperial College London</a>. This summer I had the privilege of working in Ahrefs’ mid-end team, who focus on improving developer experience of the tools the wider company uses the&nbsp;most.</p><p>In my case, this was <a href="https://dune.build/">Dune</a>. Looking over Slack discussions and GitHub issues, the core complaint I observed was difficulty understanding what Dune can see, and why it does certain things at certain times. I started gently with <a href="https://github.com/NatKarmios/dune/issues/2">some improvements to </a><a href="https://github.com/NatKarmios/dune/issues/2">dune describe</a>— these are a group of commands for reporting things that Dune knows about a project, such as build rules and external dependencies. Once I had a feel for Dune's internals, I began my main project: a “graph and trace viewer”, to explore the structure of a Dune build alongside its&nbsp;trace.</p><h3>Some Dune background</h3><p>For the rest of this to make sense, let me teach you a little about Dune’s internals. Dune relies on key abstractions over the build process; most relevant to us are dependencies and rules. <strong>Dependencies</strong> are anything that can be required to build something else, and&nbsp;include:</p><ul><li><strong>Files</strong>, such as source files to compile, input files for tests,&nbsp;etc.</li><li><strong>Directories </strong>and all the files&nbsp;within</li><li><strong>Environment variables</strong>, which can affect the build process or test execution</li><li><strong>File selectors</strong> referring to multiple files &amp; directories</li><li><strong>Aliases</strong> that expand to other dependencies</li></ul><p>Build targets are a subset of dependencies; dune build takes a list of file paths and aliases to be built, falling back to a @default alias. Then we have <strong>Rules</strong>, which declare a list of targets and, once their dependencies are built, describe the steps to build those targets with an&nbsp;Action.</p><p>Let’s suppose we have an OCaml program, sum.ml, that takes numbers from standard input and prints their sum, and we want Dune to build some files using it. We start with some rules — in their most basic form — that declare a target and describe the action to build it; this makes some files with lists of&nbsp;numbers.</p><pre>; Rule 1<br>(rule<br> (target nums1.txt)<br> (action<br>  (bash "sleep 0.25; seq 5 &gt; %{target}")))<br><br>; Rule 2<br>(rule<br> (target nums2.txt)<br> (action<br>  (bash "sleep 0.5; seq 10 10 30 &gt; %{target}")))</pre><p>Notice how the dune file syntax directly corresponds to the concepts I explained a moment ago; the rule stanza declares a rule that builds the specified target by executing the given action (with no dependencies in this&nbsp;case).</p><p>We then have rules to sum those lists, which — as specified with the deps field — depend on the list of numbers used as input, and our sum&nbsp;program.</p><pre>; Rule 3<br>(rule<br> (target sum1.txt)<br> (deps sum.exe nums1.txt)<br> (action<br>  (bash "./sum.exe &lt; nums1.txt &gt; %{target}")))<br><br>; Rule 4<br>(rule<br> (target sum2.txt)<br> (deps sum.exe nums2.txt)<br> (action<br>  (bash "./sum.exe &lt; nums2.txt &gt; %{target}")))</pre><p>Then, a rule to sum <em>those</em> results; see how the target, dependencies and action are declared implicitly this time, instead of via the target, deps and action&nbsp;fields.</p><pre>; Rule 5<br>(rule<br> (with-stdout-to total.txt<br>  (bash "cat %{dep:sum1.txt} %{dep:sum2.txt} | %{exe:sum.exe}" )))</pre><p>Finally, we need to tell Dune to build the sum program with the familiar executable stanza. Significantly more things happen implicitly here, with Dune introducing rules and dependencies not just for sum.exe, but for sum.ml, its compiled bytecode, imported libraries, and <em>even the OCaml toolchain itself</em>.</p><pre>; Rules 6+<br>(executable<br> (name sum)<br> (libraries unix))</pre><p>The build graph, then, is the directed, acyclic graph connecting all the rules and dependencies in the build; for our simple project, it’d look something like&nbsp;this:</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*mhiQvxVK4XBgqYvrm3bW0g.png" alt=""></figure><p>These abstractions — rules and dependencies — allow Dune to be remarkably concise and flexible, while offering parallelised, incremental builds. A cost of this, however, is separation between the mental model of a build (as declared in dune files), the true graph of rules and dependencies, and the build’s execution in practice. The build graph quickly grows large, obscuring the <em>why</em>, <em>when </em>and <em>how </em>of each build&nbsp;step.</p><h3>Proposal: Graph-aware build trace &amp; enhanced Perfetto&nbsp;UI</h3><p>Here’s my attempt to address this problem: an enhancement of Dune’s existing trace infrastructure, and an extension of the Perfetto trace viewer integrating Dune’s build&nbsp;graph.</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*5uf5_-B8L23iRyORfbrIMw.png" alt=""><figcaption>The Dune-aware Perfetto trace viewer, exploring the trace of building&nbsp;<a href="https://github.com/ocaml/merlin">Merlin</a>.</figcaption></figure><p>To use it, run a Dune build with graph tracing enabled and output a Perfetto&nbsp;trace.</p><pre>$ DUNE_TRACE=+graph dune build @target<br>$ dune trace perfetto -o my-trace.perfetto</pre><p>Then, load the result in our modified Perfetto UI. In addition to Perfetto’s standard offerings, this trace viewer provides a number of Dune-specific features, including:</p><ul><li>Information about the relevant rule, dependency, or build directory is shown when selecting relevant trace&nbsp;events</li><li>Build-graph information can be queried alongside the trace via Perfetto’s SQL capabilities</li><li>First-class support for Dune data in Perfetto’s Data Explorer (experimental), to create and reuse complex queries and data visualisations</li></ul><p>Check out the screencast below for a guided tour of the interface. For more information (and the source code), check out our forks of <a href="https://github.com/ahrefs/dune/blob/trace-graph/doc/dev/trace-graph-perfetto.md">Dune</a> and <a href="https://github.com/ahrefs/perfetto/tree/dune-graph-trace/ui/src/plugins/com.karmios.nat.DuneGraph">Perfetto</a>.</p><iframe src="https://cdn.embedly.com/widgets/media.html?src=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fembed%2Fp3i0pkn5eFs%3Ffeature%3Doembed&amp;display_name=YouTube&amp;url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fwatch%3Fv%3Dp3i0pkn5eFs&amp;image=https%3A%2F%2Fi.ytimg.com%2Fvi%2Fp3i0pkn5eFs%2Fhqdefault.jpg&amp;type=text%2Fhtml&amp;schema=youtube" width="854" height="480" frameborder="0" scrolling="no"><a href="https://medium.com/media/77fe83a9709599d244d67665239889ea/href">https://medium.com/media/77fe83a9709599d244d67665239889ea/href</a></iframe><h3>Crossing the&nbsp;desert</h3><p>Of course, there were many twists and turns in making this tooling a reality, so please enjoy an abridged account of my summer’s work. There are three key areas of implementation: reporting the build graph in Dune’s trace, importing it all into Perfetto, and extending Perfetto’s UI to make it&nbsp;useful.</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*9quREy-hlhpiLfswzryk6A.png" alt=""></figure><h4>Emitting traces in&nbsp;Dune</h4><p>The first step is getting Dune to report the build graph. Rather than creating a new channel for this data, I used Dune’s <a href="https://dune.readthedocs.io/en/stable/advanced/profiling-dune.html">existing tracing infrastructure</a>, where messages are logged to an internal file and later converted to a readable format. This was generally harmonious with Dune’s internals, though there were a couple of interesting hitches owing to Dune’s tendency to be incredibly lazy: the majority of the codebase uses a monad for delayed execution (to put off work until it’s actually needed) and memoization (to avoid doing the same work&nbsp;twice).</p><p>While a dependency build or a rule execution can be required by many dependants, I thought it’d be useful to report the “forcer” of each of these — the single thing that first requested them. This is naturally racy, non-deterministic data, so how can one thread it through the memoized functions at the center of the build engine? As it turns out, the core monad for organising execution is also a reader monad, allowing variables to be set for nested computations; this is mainly used for error handlers, but it fits our purposes nicely, if arguably a bodge. Each rule and dependency declares itself the forcer of anything built under it, only propagating through to operations that haven’t yet been triggered.</p><p>When considering failing builds, however, Dune’s laziness really got in the way. Internally, a rule doesn’t list its dependencies upfront: they can only be determined by evaluating the “action builder”, which collects dependencies as they’re required. If building a dependency fails, then the resulting exception prevents the final dependency set from being reported; this severs that target and all its transitive dependents from the build graph, making it practically useless. I’ve come up with a couple of solutions to this, though I’m not particularly satisfied with&nbsp;either.</p><p>My first response was to try and recover the dependencies with the action builder’s lazy mode, which in most cases, collects dependencies without building them. The problem is with that “most”, as this creates fringe cases where the lazy pass can progress further than the eager one and trigger a build, meaning the build result would be different when graph tracing is&nbsp;enabled.</p><p>My other approach was to introduce a third “eager-reaching” evaluation mode for the action builder, which remembers the result (including exceptions) and the encountered dependencies of memoized steps, reporting the dependencies even on failure. This prevents the overshooting problem, but it can result in a little bit of repeated work if an action builder is evaluated in both eager and eager-reaching mode. Also, call it superstition or impostor syndrome, but making this kind of change to such a core part of dune just has… bad vibes? Maintainers, feel free to tell me&nbsp;off.</p><h4>Consuming in&nbsp;Perfetto</h4><p>With Dune reporting the build graph, the next concern is the interchange between Dune and Perfetto. My instinct was to use the JSON Chrome trace output that Dune already supports. This led to some problems, largely stemming from the trace format being quite loosely defined — the only official documentation I could find is <a href="https://docs.google.com/document/d/1CvAClvFfyA5R-PhYUmn5OOQtYMH4h6I0nSsKchNAySU/preview?tab=t.0#heading=h.yr4qxyxotyw">a relatively brief Google Doc</a>. The biggest issue is overlapping event durations. The documentation specifies that trace events should only overlap when properly nested, which makes sense for synchronous per-thread traces, but not for something as deeply asynchronous as Dune. The documentation does mention asynchronous events, but <a href="https://github.com/google/perfetto/issues/60#issuecomment-690140807">Perfetto doesn’t support it when importing from Chrome trace&nbsp;JSON</a>.</p><p>So, I thought, why not speak to Perfetto in its own language? That being, <a href="https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:third_party/perfetto/protos/perfetto/trace/track_event/track_event.proto">its protobuf</a>. It gives more fine-grained control over trace output, integrates better with Perfetto, and, as will soon become relevant, gives more opportunities to optimise performance. The question then became, how? While ample OCaml tooling for protobuf exists, Dune is deliberately light on dependencies as it has to be able to bootstrap itself. On the other hand, given my time constraints, it’s not as though I could hand-roll an encoder&nbsp;myself!</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*0sBzWTANKhPjXzWgt6hTZA.png" alt=""></figure><p>This is where Claude interjected and, despite my scepticism, one-shot an encoder in about half an hour. If a dependency-free Perfetto protobuf encoder is useful to anyone else, <a href="https://github.com/NatKarmios/ocaml-perfetto">I’ve hosted the library separately</a>.</p><p>Now that we have all our trace data, complete with the build graph, we can finally start <em>looking</em> at the traces we’ve&nbsp;made…</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*xmJQy-uYSW_NMFfOR5N-JA.gif" alt=""></figure><p>…in juuust a&nbsp;moment…</p><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*GtPTGcfNW3dKMYbIa_GO8w.png" alt=""></figure><figure><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/751/0*cFC3BBngzEzGEOWU" alt=""></figure><p>…ah.</p><p>What was Perfetto doing that whole time? Well, Perfetto tries to process every debug field on every event—which I use to carry build-graph data-for later retrieval — into an “arg set” register. Then, in the Dune plugin, we try to pour all that build-graph data into Perfetto’s SQL engine, in a shape that’s useful to us. My target for this work is Ahrefs’ monorepo, and I shouldn’t have been surprised that Perfetto couldn’t handle a standard CI build trace — weighing in at 2.5GB at this point — without some serious optimisation. We tackle these inefficiencies in Dune, when converting the&nbsp;trace.</p><p>First, we scrape the build graph data from individual rule and dependency events — leaving them with the bare minimum to associate with the relevant rule or dependency — and collect it into a “build-graph blob”, which is emitted in chunks on special instant events. This also includes an intern table, cutting down on duplicated strings.</p><p>Second, a lot of the data overwhelm is caused by the sheer number of edges in the build-graph; about 2.8 million on my monorepo trace. Many dependency sets tend to be similar, but not quite identical. The obvious response is to delta-encode dependency sets (expressing them as additions and removals to other sets), but this would require a slow recursive query to resolve dependency edges and make reverse dependencies impossible to follow. Instead, we find core sets of dependencies shared between multiple rules, and express the resulting dependency sets as additions to those cores. This gives a 6x space saving; it’s not as strong as delta-encoding’s 20x, but a worthwhile trade to keep edge queries performant.</p><p>These changes, combined with string interning at the protocol level and gzipping the result — both of which Perfetto natively supports — cut that trace from 2.5GB to under 90MB, fully loading in about a&nbsp;minute.</p><h4>Extending Perfetto’s UI</h4><p>At last, we can think about the interface for actually exploring the data. I find that iterating on UI can be quite time intensive, and I’m not familiar with the <a href="https://mithril.js.org/">Mithril</a> framework that Perfetto uses, so I leaned a bit more heavily on Claude for this&nbsp;part.</p><p>The Perfetto UI is an odd beast when it comes to extensibility; it supports plugins, but <a href="https://perfetto.dev/docs/visualization/extending-the-ui">they must live in the source tree</a>. A lot of the UI was pretty close to what I wanted, but not quite. Some areas (like the data explorer) needed some extra extension points to work, where others (like the SQL query interface) were more economical to just clone with the desired tweaks. I did keep <a href="https://github.com/NatKarmios/perfetto/blob/dune-graph-trace/ui/src/plugins/com.karmios.nat.DuneGraph/UPSTREAM.md">a log of upstream changes</a> for the sake of hypothetical pull requests but, in retrospect, it’d probably make the most sense to stop worrying about the boundary between the Dune plugin and the rest of Perfetto, and turn it into a “Dune trace viewer” hard fork. This is, in fact, just what Jane Street did with <a href="https://github.com/janestreet/magic-trace">magic-trace</a>.</p><h3>In conclusion</h3><p>Reflecting on the project, the strongest use found so far is diagnosing parallelism bottlenecks; there’s the lwt example shown in the screencast, but also a real issue discovered in the Ahrefs monorepo. What some colleagues observed as Dune sometimes stalling at random likely turned out to be a bunch of executables linking at once, and throttling the dev server's write buffer (I discovered this when I made a dev server unusable for everyone else one morning 🙂). It was precisely because the graph trace revealed which processes stalled, and <em>what targets their rules were building</em>, that we discovered the root&nbsp;cause.</p><p>The hope is, of course, that this work can be more widely useful for understanding and optimising Dune builds. Bear in mind my constraints in this&nbsp;project:</p><ul><li>Three months is pretty&nbsp;tight!</li><li>While I have some familiarity with build systems, I am by no means an expert in&nbsp;them.</li><li>I’m also new to Ahrefs, and a monorepo like theirs will naturally be too nuanced for an intern to get a handle on (I recall one of the onboarding instructions was “Do not even try to understand our system architecture”).</li></ul><p>This was what made the Perfetto approach attractive; I could focus on providing all the relevant data — at the scale of the monorepo — and laying the groundwork to explore it for those who come&nbsp;after.</p><p>Naturally, these constraints mean there are still some gaps to be filled. A large one is tracing watch-mode and multi-context builds; currently, everything assumes each rule and dependency is handled once. There are also some edge cases for what triggers builds: most happen because of rule dependencies, but there are a number of scenarios like dune files with variable expansion and dynamic-includes. It’d also be nice to perform some amount of headless analysis on a trace: for example, CI could flag when a build parallelises less than a previous one, and point to the rules that might be responsible. Last, and most important, is upstreaming; requiring people to pin their Dune isn’t particularly sustainable, but this work makes some non-trivial changes to a codebase that is, quite rightfully, developed with&nbsp;caution.</p><p>I’d like to thank my friend and colleague Sacha Ayoun, not just for his contributions to the OCaml community, but for his unyielding personal support in entering this internship and beyond. Of course, huge thanks go to Ahrefs for giving me this opportunity to work with such an amazing team. I was generously invited to join the company at this year’s OCaml workshop in Paris to meet some of my colleagues in person, and it was wonderful to find some fellow Nix d̶e̶g̶e̶n̶e̶r̶a̶t̶e̶s enthusiasts. Finally, I thank my mentors, Louis Roché and Raphael Proust, for creating an environment in which I felt reassured and empowered to see this project&nbsp;through.</p><img src="https://medium.com/_/stat?event=post.clientViewed&amp;referrerSource=full_rss&amp;postId=56603d7794bc" width="1" height="1" alt=""><hr><p><a href="https://tech.ahrefs.com/tracing-dune-with-a-side-of-graphs-56603d7794bc">Tracing Dune, with a side of graphs</a> was originally published in <a href="https://tech.ahrefs.com">Ahrefs</a> on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.</p>
